Infertility after Abortion

1:2 matched case-control study, real data

The study

Trichopoulos and colleagues studied whether induced and spontaneous abortions increase the risk of secondary infertility (Trichopoulos et al. 1976). Each of 83 infertile women (the cases) was matched with two fertile women (the controls) of the same age, parity and education. One set has a single control. The data are R’s infert, and they ship with the module: in jamovi, Open ▸ Data Library ▸ Conditional Logistic Regression ▸ Infertility after abortion. Or download infert.csv.

Variable Content
stratum matched set, 1–83
case 1 = infertile (case), 0 = fertile (control)
spontaneous, induced number of previous spontaneous / induced abortions (0, 1, 2 or more)
age, parity, education the matching factors
pooled_stratum a coarser grouping of the sets, not used here

The basic analysis

  1. Open the data and choose Analyses ▸ Regression ▸ Logistic Regression ▸ Conditional (Matched).
  2. Move case to Dependent Variable, stratum to Matching Variable, and spontaneous and induced to Covariates.
  3. In Model Coefficients, tick Odds ratio and its Confidence interval. In Model Fit, tick Overall model test.

The matched-set table confirms the design: 82 sets of a case and two controls, one set with a single control, all informative. Each spontaneous abortion multiplies the odds of infertility by 7.29 (95% CI 3.65–14.54), and each induced abortion by 4.09 (2.02–8.30). Both effects hold among women of the same age, parity and education.

A count as a factor

Entering the number of abortions as a covariate assumes the same odds ratio for each additional one. To relax that for spontaneous abortions, enter it as a factor. In jamovi, spontaneous is a nominal integer, so it can go into Factors. Put induced in block 1 and the factor in block 2 of the Model Builder, so that the Model Comparisons table tests its addition. Then tick Likelihood ratio tests for the omnibus test of the factor.

Against women with no spontaneous abortion, one multiplies the odds by 7.7 and two or more by 51.1 (12.4–211.1). The covariate coding implied 7.3 for one and 7.3² ≈ 53 for two. The factor agrees with it, so the simpler per-abortion odds ratio describes these data well.

Matching factors and effect modification

Age was matched on. Add age to Covariates and to the model, and also the interaction induced * age: select both variables in the Model Builder and choose Interaction from the arrow’s menu.

Note. A term with no estimable coefficient (e.g. a matching factor) is not tested

age has no coefficient and no test. It is constant within every matched set, so it cancels from the conditional likelihood, and the note says so. The interaction is still estimable, because it asks whether the effect of induced abortions differs between sets of different ages, and that is a within-set contrast. Here there is no evidence of it (p = .579). With the interaction in the model, the induced row is the odds ratio at age 0, which has no practical meaning. Look at the interaction test, not at that row.

What if the matching is ignored?

The same coefficients from ordinary logistic regression (R’s glm) of the same data:

Analysis spontaneous OR induced OR
Conditional (this module) 7.29 4.09
Unconditional, matching ignored 3.31 1.52
Unconditional, one indicator per set 25.29 8.94
Unconditional, adjusted for age, parity and education 7.74 3.63

Ignoring the matching shrinks both odds ratios to less than half, because the matching factors are related to the exposures. An indicator per matched set inflates them several-fold, which is the bias described in the method. Adjusting an unconditional model for the matching factors comes closer here, because the matching was on a few broad categories. The conditional analysis needs no such luck.
